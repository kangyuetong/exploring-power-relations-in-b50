Palm oil · biodiesel · political economy

Exploring power chains behind Indonesia's move from B40 to B50

Indonesia turns palm oil into transport fuel to reduce dependence on petroleum diesel. This atlas follows the crop, the money and the decisions behind that strategy—and asks whether the transition can deliver energy security without shifting environmental costs onto less powerful actors.

01 / The mandate

First, how does palm oil become biodiesel?

Oil palm fruit is processed into crude palm oil, which can be refined and chemically converted into fatty acid methyl esters (FAME). This biodiesel is blended with conventional petroleum diesel. Indonesia is the world's largest palm oil producer, so its established plantations, mills and refining industry make palm an accessible domestic feedstock—but also connect fuel policy to land use, food markets and commodity exports.

B40 means 40% biodiesel and 60% petroleum diesel by volume. It was the mandated blend in 2025.

Why pursue the mandate?

Why Indonesia uses palm oil instead of only imported diesel

Indonesia already produces large quantities of palm oil and has mills, refineries and biodiesel factories. Turning part of that supply into fuel helps replace imported petroleum diesel and creates a domestic buyer for palm oil. The trade-off is that the same crop is also used in food and exports. More fuel demand can increase pressure on prices, farmland and forests unless supply grows through better yields and stronger safeguards. B50 changes the required blend, not automatically the amount of new land planted.

ESDM: policy rationale ↗

The underlying supply chain did not suddenly change at B50. Indonesia already regulated blending and supported biodiesel before 2026. The larger mandated share changes the pressure on this existing system.

02 / The underlying controversy

Why has palm oil been so contested in Indonesia?

Oil palm produces a high quantity of vegetable oil per hectare compared with many alternatives. The controversy is not that the crop is inherently harmful: it is how plantations have expanded, whose land was converted, and who captures the benefits. Biodiesel links those long-running conflicts to national energy policy.

FORESTS & PEATLAND

Conversion can create a large carbon debt

Clearing forests removes habitat and carbon stores. Draining peat soils can release stored carbon and increase fire vulnerability. A litre of biodiesel is not automatically low-carbon if its feedstock is associated with major land-use emissions.

Why B50 matters: additional feedstock demand may create pressure for expansion unless productivity and land safeguards hold.

Global Forest Watch · Indonesia ↗
LAND & LIVELIHOODS

Plantation growth can intensify tenure disputes

Plantation concessions can overlap customary land claims or areas used by local communities. Benefits such as jobs and market access can coexist with disputes over consent, compensation, water access and labour conditions.

Why B50 matters: increased demand does not itself establish that any particular dispute was caused by biodiesel.

Human Rights Watch · Indonesia cases ↗
FOOD & TRADE

One commodity serves competing markets

Palm oil is used in cooking oil, packaged foods, industrial products and biodiesel. When policy directs more feedstock into fuel, its effects on domestic prices and exports depend on harvests, stocks, trade policy and global prices.

Why B50 matters: the opportunity cost of fuel demand becomes an explicit governance question.

IEA · biofuels context ↗

What is the causal question?

Indonesia's palm-oil sector had deforestation, tenure and labour controversies long before B40. To evaluate the biodiesel mandate fairly, we must distinguish harms attributable to palm-oil production generally from additional pressures created by biodiesel demand, and from future outcomes that are only modelled.

03 / Policy chronology

Why did Indonesia keep increasing its biodiesel mandate?

Indonesia began mandatory biodiesel blending well before B40. Its policy evolved from creating a market for domestic palm-derived fuel to making that fuel a larger part of national diesel supply. This strengthened demand for producers, but also made feedstock availability, export-levy revenues, fuel quality and land-use safeguards increasingly important.

Before 2025

Years of mandates built a subsidised biodiesel market

Indonesia introduced progressively higher blending requirements over successive years. The export-levy-funded incentive mechanism helped eligible biodiesel suppliers cover part of the difference between biodiesel and petroleum-diesel reference prices. This matters because a mandate creates buyers, while financing determines which producers can supply them economically.

2025 · B40

B40 expanded a guaranteed market—and its funding burden

B40 took effect in 2025 and increased the share of FAME required in blended diesel. It expanded guaranteed domestic demand for palm-derived fuel, but producers still needed feedstock, fuel distributors needed reliable logistics, and the plantation fund needed sufficient levy revenue to support eligible supply. The central question is whether increased volume was matched by stronger environmental safeguards.

2026 · B50

B50 raises the stakes for supply and financing

B50 raises the FAME share to half of each litre of blended fuel. It does not create a new supply chain: it places greater demands on the existing one. That can reduce petroleum-diesel requirements, but may intensify competition for palm oil, pressure on the incentive fund and the importance of verifying plantation origin. These risks are conditional, not automatically observed damage.

The distinction matters: announcing a mandate, beginning distribution and achieving full nationwide coverage are different milestones.

04 / Follow the power

Defining power with Steven Lukes

The physical product moves from growers to mills and fuel producers. Money follows a different route, including commercial payments and a palm-export-levy-funded incentive scheme. Authority flows through government mandates, financing rules and enforcement. Using Steven Lukes, power means more than making decisions: it also means keeping some issues off the agenda and shaping what people accept as legitimate. We assess actors through formal authority, control over finance or markets, and influence over the public narrative. These are qualitative interpretations, not measured scores.

The theoretical lens · Steven Lukes (1974; revised 2005)

1. Decisions

Who sets B50, determines eligibility and enforces the rules? Government has formal authority, while firms negotiate how the rules are implemented.

2. The agenda

Which concerns receive priority? Diesel imports and production volumes are visible policy goals; land rights, food prices and smallholder costs may receive less attention.

3. Framing

Who defines what counts as a successful transition? Energy-security narratives and environmental critiques compete to shape the meaning of “sustainable fuel.”

05 / The evidence

What happened under B40—and what could B50 make worse?

B40 expanded domestic biodiesel use, but it operated within a palm-oil sector already facing land, financing and distributional controversies. The cards separate observed outcomes, disputes and projected risks. Hover to lift and click to reveal an expanded explanation, the causal connection and why it matters for B50. The transition to B50

B40 exposed trade-offs. B50 increases their potential scale.

Government reporting shows B50 distribution began in July 2026. By early September, roughly 94% of stations were distributing it, while some terminals were still completing the transition. Whether higher blending causes additional deforestation, food-price pressure or fiscal strain depends on supply responses and safeguards; these are risks to investigate, not predetermined outcomes.

From diagnosis to institutional design

Ansell & Gash: collaboration needs more than inviting everyone into the room

Ansell and Gash (2008) describe collaborative governance as formal, consensus-oriented engagement among public agencies and non-state actors. Their model highlights starting conditions, power and resource imbalances, institutional design, facilitative leadership, and trust-building through repeated dialogue. Applied to B50, a forum dominated by ministries and large producers could reproduce existing inequalities even if smallholders are nominally represented.

Representation

Reserve meaningful seats for independent smallholders, affected communities, NGOs, fuel producers and public agencies; publish agendas and decisions.

Resources

Fund translation, travel, legal and technical assistance so participation does not depend on a stakeholder's financial capacity.

Accountability

Connect joint decisions to published sourcing data, grievance channels, independent audits and time-bound enforcement.

Theory: Ansell, C. & Gash, A. (2008), “Collaborative Governance in Theory and Practice,” Journal of Public Administration Research and Theory 18(4), 543–571. This is a proposed application, not an existing Indonesian B50 institution.
